Ecommerce Analytics: A Practical Guide to Connecting Store, Ad, and Finance Data

Hologrow · AI-assisted editorialPublished October 10, 20265 min read

Ecommerce analytics turns order, advertising, customer, and cost data into decisions about growth and profitability. Start by defining the business question, then align reporting periods, currencies, identifiers, and metric definitions. A connected data layer can make those inputs reusable, but it cannot replace missing costs, sound analysis, or human judgment.

Key takeaways

  • Choose a decision before choosing a dashboard.
  • Aggregate each source to a compatible grain before joining it.
  • Keep store revenue, attributed revenue, contribution, and cash separate.

What should ecommerce analytics actually help you decide?

The useful starting point is not “How many metrics can we track?” It is “What would we do differently if we knew the answer?” A US merchant selling on Amazon and a direct-to-consumer store might need to fund a promotion, replenish a product, or investigate a deteriorating margin. Those decisions need different inputs.

For a promotion review, begin with fulfilled demand, discounts, returns, product costs, and incremental marketing expense. For a checkout investigation, start with sessions and funnel events. For a cash question, examine payment and settlement timing. Putting all three on one chart does not make them interchangeable.

Write a decision contract: “On Monday, the growth lead will decide whether to maintain the current promotion using two complete weeks of net sales and variable contribution.” Name an owner, a cutoff, and the evidence that would change the decision. This is the first deliverable; the dashboard comes later.

Which data do you need from stores, advertising, and finance?

Use a small data contract before connecting more systems. The fields below describe a recommended analytical model, not a promise that every connector exposes these exact column names. Map them to the actual schema and preserve source identifiers.

One row per order lineStore/account, order ID, line ID, SKU, status, event time, currency, sales, discountWhat was sold, where, and when?
Campaign × reporting dayAccount, campaign, date, spend, clicks, attributed sales, attribution settingWhat demand did we pay to acquire?

An order line and a campaign-day are different units of observation, often called their grain. Never join every order directly to every campaign on date alone. Ten order lines and three campaigns can produce thirty rows. Aggregate first to a justified shared level, such as channel and business day, or use an explicitly documented allocation method.

How do you reconcile revenue without counting it twice?

Give each revenue measure a separate name. Store net merchandise sales answer an order-economics question. An advertising platform’s attributed sales answer a credit-allocation question. Payouts answer a cash-timing question. Add neither ad-attributed sales to store sales nor payouts to revenue.

For this management view, define net merchandise sales as gross merchandise sales less discounts and returns, excluding tax and shipping revenue. This is a chosen convention, not a universal platform or accounting definition. Keep shipping revenue and related costs as explicit lines when material. Finance should approve the convention before it becomes recurring.

Use three reconciliation tests: totals before versus after joins; missing or duplicated business keys; and date boundaries around refunds and cancellations. A reconciliation bridge can contain an unexplained residual. Showing that residual is better than forcing agreement through an undocumented adjustment.

What does a connected profit analysis look like?

Consider this synthetic monthly example in USD. Costs follow the same management reporting convention; these are not customer results or an audited income statement.

Illustrative contribution bridge—not customer data
LineAmount
Net merchandise sales$120,000
Product costs−$42,000
Variable fulfillment and transaction costs−$18,000
Advertising−$30,000
Contribution before fixed overhead$30,000

Contribution is $120,000 − $42,000 − $18,000 − $30,000 = $30,000, or 25% of net merchandise sales. If the cost file covers only 80% of units, do not publish that margin as complete. Show the covered share, uncosted units, and a clearly labeled estimate or range.

Next investigate volume, net price, product mix, unit cost, fulfillment, and advertising. Aggregate totals point to an investigation; they do not establish causation. A cheaper product mix can lower average order value even when no individual product’s price changes.

How should you ask AI for a source-backed answer?

Run two passes. First validate scope and arithmetic. Then request explanations the evidence supports. Reject an answer that turns correlation into causation or substitutes attributed sales for actual orders.

A weekly review needs four outputs: a decision, its evidence, an unresolved question, and a named next action. Preserve that packet so the following week starts from consistent definitions rather than another debate about what “revenue” means.

A question to ask your AI

For our US business, compare the last two complete reporting weeks. First list accounts, table coverage, time zones, and freshness. Reconcile net merchandise sales before analyzing contribution. State the cost definition and uncosted share. Return the three largest changes with sources, calculations, uncertainty, and the next evidence needed. Do not modify source accounts.
